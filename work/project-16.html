Overview

Glossy Island is an interactive installation exploring how proximity changes the way a material reveals itself. Translucent fluorescent acrylic — driven by servo motors and ultrasonic proximity sensing — shifts its angle so that light passes through, reflects from, and overlaps across the colored surfaces differently as you move. You don't simply look at the piece; your position becomes part of its behavior.

"The closer you get to me, the more of my colorful experiences you see."

The System

The installation reads as four connected layers. The technology is intentionally secondary — the real interface is the space between the viewer and the object.

Signal chain · four layers

  1. 01Sensing — an ultrasonic sensor observes the viewer's proximity
  2. 02Computation — a Raspberry Pi Pico filters the data and maps distance to behavior
  3. 03Motion — micro servos rotate the acrylic, with speed following closeness
  4. 04Optics — fluorescent acrylic turns that motion into color, reflection, overlap & shadow
Concept & Material

The project began as a wall-mounted light installation — roughly twenty translucent fluorescent shards on hidden servos, calm when nobody was near and awakening as a viewer approached. Triangles became the core geometry: three strong edges, a silhouette that changes dramatically when rotated, and clear overlapping color relationships. The acrylic was specified as 3 mm translucent fluorescent sheet in pink, yellow, orange, cyan, green, and purple.

Rather than building twenty unique mechanical objects, the guiding principle became mechanical repetition + visual variation — one consistent module, with variety coming from acrylic shape, color, scale, orientation, and programmed movement.

Mechanical Development

The first physical question was how to attach a 3 mm acrylic triangle to a tiny 9 g servo without cracking it. Mounting directly to the shaft concentrated stress at a single point, so the connection evolved into a modular chain:

Mounting chain

  1. 01Servo spline
  2. 02Servo horn
  3. 03Adapter plate
  4. 04Acrylic (two holes, M3 + nylon washers)

This became a standardized, 3D-printed servo module: the same mechanical unit repeats while the visible acrylic changes, and a failed servo never means rebuilding the whole piece. SG90 servos were used for prototyping; the metal-gear MG90S was chosen for the durable final build, with a bearing-supported axle considered for longer exhibition runs.

Electronics & First Prototype

A 20-servo Arduino diagram was useful as a systems study, but the project was deliberately reduced to the smallest functional prototype — one Raspberry Pi Pico H, one HC-SR04 sensor, one servo, one acrylic element — so the core interaction could be tested before scaling.

Pin Map

GP0 · GP2 · GP3

GP0 → servo signal, GP2 → HC-SR04 TRIG, GP3 → ECHO, behind a 1 kΩ / 2 kΩ divider stepping the 5 V echo down to the Pico's 3.3 V logic.

Debug

It was CircuitPython

An ImportError: no module named machine and a CIRCUITPY drive revealed the board ran CircuitPython — rewritten with board, pwmio and adafruit_hcsr04.

Interaction & Tuning

Raw ultrasonic data is noisy — a stationary viewer reads 38–41 cm and the acrylic twitches. Median sampling, exponential smoothing, angle deadbands, and NO-ECHO tolerance made the piece react to meaningful human movement, not measurement noise. The key decision was mapping distance to oscillation speed rather than position, along a nonlinear closeness curve that accelerates as you approach.

Less is more: motion was clamped to roughly 5°–175° to avoid servo buzz and stall, and the maximum speed was later reduced substantially — very fast movement made the piece feel mechanical rather than spatial. Because transparent acrylic redirects light with only a small angular change, large optical shifts never needed large rotations.
Scaling & Power

Moving from one servo to five exposed a system-level constraint: several servos drawing current at startup, direction changes, or stall destabilized the USB-powered breadboard. The fix reshaped the architecture — the Pico handles logic and PWM only, while a dedicated regulated 5 V supply powers the servos through its own distribution rail, with a shared common ground. That shift moved the project from an electronics demo toward a system that could realistically scale.

From Wall to Island

Midway, the installation changed form entirely. Instead of a wall of ~20 independent triangles, it became a freestanding tabletop landscape of five vertical "trees," each carrying three to four acrylic elements, on a layered ~27 × 17 inch wooden base whose descending profiles create an inverted, floating-island topography. The geometry opened up from pure triangles toward mixed angular and organic silhouettes — and that shift named the piece.

The final build combined five fabrication processes: woodworking for the island, laser cutting for the fluorescent acrylic, 3D printing for servo mounts, physical computing on the Pico, and servo programming for responsive motion. A later NeoPixel lighting test used a quiet warm-gold fade — illumination that complements the acrylic rather than overpowering it, echoing the whole project's principle: the technology stays quiet while amplifying the material.

Summary & Reflection
Before

"The problem is making acrylic move: detect a person → rotate a shard → cast colored shadows."

After

"The problem is how movement should feel — and how the technology can disappear into the material it serves."

Glossy Island became an exercise in translating an abstract emotional idea into a physical feedback system, moving repeatedly between composition, mechanics, electronics, programming, and fabrication. Each prototype removed uncertainty from one layer — how distance becomes motion, how much movement is enough, how to read noisy sensing, how mechanisms can vanish into an artwork. The final experience is intentionally simple: from a distance, you see the island; as you approach, the island begins to reveal itself.
