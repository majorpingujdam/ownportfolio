Overview

Glossy Island is a proximity-driven kinetic light installation. Fluorescent acrylic forms, carried on servo-driven armatures and governed by ultrasonic sensing, change how they catch and throw light as a viewer approaches — turning the audience's distance into the instrument that plays the piece. From across the room it reads as a still composition; step toward it and the shapes begin to turn, overlapping color and sliding shadow across the surfaces until the object feels alive.

The experience is written as one line of behavior: from a distance, the island waits; your presence begins the movement; and the closer you come, the more of its color you see.

"If you come closer to me, you will see my colorful experiences."

Design Challenge

The brief was deceptively open — make a material respond to people. Moving a piece of acrylic with a motor is trivial; the actual design problem was translation: turning a crude, noisy distance reading into motion that reads as alive and intentional rather than mechanical and twitchy. Four tensions defined the work.

Legibility

Noise vs. intent

Ultrasonic distance jitters several centimetres even when nobody moves. The piece had to respond to human presence, not to measurement error.

Feel

Spatial vs. robotic

Direct distance-to-angle mapping felt like a machine arm. The motion needed a curve that read as breathing and acceleration, not stepping.

Restraint

Visible vs. invisible tech

The subject is color, light and material — so the servos, wiring and controller had to recede and let the acrylic do the talking.

Scale

One vs. many

A single module was easy; a field of them introduced power, repeatability and maintenance problems that a one-off prototype never surfaces.

The Interaction Model

Rather than map distance to position, I mapped it to speed along a nonlinear closeness curve — gentle change far away, sharp speed-up up close — and split the field into three zones. The result is a clear cause-and-effect the body understands without instruction: approach and the piece quickens; retreat and it calms; cross the threshold and it rests.

Max Zone

< 30 cm

Capped at ~40°/s. Close enough that extra speed would feel frantic — so motion is held at its expressive ceiling.

Ramp Zone

30 – 220 cm

Closer = faster, on a curve. Speed climbs from a near-still drift to the ceiling as the viewer closes the gap.

Stop Zone

> 220 cm

The piece rests. A deliberate hard cut from ~3°/s to 0 — a decisive "off", not a lingering fade.

Felt time, not just angle. One full sweep (5°→175°) takes ~4.3 s when you're within 0.3 m, ~8.5 s at 1 m, ~17 s at 1.5 m and ~45 s at 2 m — so the same gesture reads as urgent up close and almost meditative from across the room. Motion was clamped to 5°–175° to avoid servo buzz, and the top speed was pulled back on purpose: fast looked mechanical.
Process

The piece came together as three parallel builds — a circuit, a structure, and a material — each prototyped on its own before they met.

System & Build

Under the color sits a deliberately quiet four-layer chain. Each layer was de-risked on a single module before the field was assembled.

Signal chain

  1. 01Sensing — an ultrasonic sensor reads the viewer's proximity
  2. 02Computation — a Raspberry Pi Pico filters the data and maps distance to speed
  3. 03Motion — micro-servos rotate the acrylic, pace following closeness
  4. 04Optics — fluorescent acrylic turns motion into color, reflection & shadow
Mechanism

Modular, not fragile

Acrylic mounts through a servo → horn → adapter chain (two fixing points, not one) so a 3 mm sheet never cracks, and a failed servo swaps out without rebuilding the piece. MG90S metal-gear for the final build.

Signal & Power

Logic ≠ power

A 1 kΩ/2 kΩ divider drops the 5 V echo to the Pico's 3.3 V logic; and when one servo became five, motor current moved off the board onto a dedicated regulated 5 V rail with a shared ground.

Debug worth keeping: the controller shipped running CircuitPython, not MicroPython — surfaced as an ImportError: no module named machine and a CIRCUITPY drive. Rewriting against board, pwmio and adafruit_hcsr04 got clean distance reads.
From Wall to Island

The concept started as a ~20-shard interactive wall. Prototyping pushed it somewhere better: a freestanding, layered wooden base — a floating topography — carrying vertical clusters of acrylic. One island became a small archipelago of sculptures, each a different arrangement of forms, wired back to a shared sensing hub. The mechanism recedes into the base; what you read is an organic landscape that happens to breathe when you approach. That shift is what named the piece.

Reflection
Before

"The problem is making acrylic move: detect a person → rotate a shard → cast colored shadows."

After

"The problem is how movement should feel — and how the technology can disappear into the material it serves."

Glossy Island was an exercise in translating an emotional idea into a physical feedback system, moving continuously between composition, mechanics, electronics and fabrication. The decisions that mattered weren't the obvious ones — they were the smoothing curve, the capped top speed, the hard stop, the mounts that vanish. The final experience is intentionally simple: from a distance, you see the island; as you approach, the island begins to reveal itself.
